Case Study

Boost Mobile — subscriber economics and retention.

EchoStar's Boost Mobile is a useful real-world case for marketing-driven unit economics because its public filings show a brand that almost didn't make it. Since EchoStar took over the carrier, it lost roughly 2 million subscribers through 2024 before turning a corner: net additions returned in 2025, with +150K in Q1, +212K in Q2, and +223K in Q3, bringing the base to roughly 7.5 million subscribers. Full-year 2025 wireless churn improved to 2.84%, down from 3.00% in 2024, while ARPU grew 2.3% to $37.41. What follows is my own modeled read on what's driving that turnaround and where the economics could still improve, built from Boost's disclosures plus my own assumptions wherever the company doesn't break out the underlying marketing figures.

To estimate acquisition economics, I modeled a blended contribution margin of ~50% on service revenue after network and support costs, and a CAC of roughly $180 per gross add — toward the lower end of typical prepaid carrier CAC, given Boost's growing mix of lower-cost digital-channel sales. At a 2.84% monthly churn rate, that implies an average subscriber lifetime near 35 months. Working through the model: LTV ≈ $37.41 × 50% × 35 months ≈ $655; payback period ≈ $180 ÷ ($37.41 × 50%) ≈ 9.6 months; and acquisition ROI lands near 2.6x (LTV:CAC). None of those three are disclosed by EchoStar directly — they're my own build from the company's reported ARPU and churn.

On why churn actually moved: Boost's own disclosures point to two real levers behind the FY2025 improvement — a management-stated shift toward acquiring and retaining "higher quality, long-term subscribers," and continued growth in self-pay digital-channel sales, which likely carry a materially lower CAC than the dealer/retail channel (the company specifically calls out dealer incentive costs as a drag). That matches a broader pattern in prepaid wireless: subsidized or heavily discounted acquisition channels bring in subscribers who churn fast, while self-pay digital subscribers tend to stick around longer. The 2025 subscriber mix shift — including higher government-subsidized growth replacing the lost Affordable Connectivity Program base — suggests Boost is actively managing which segments it leans into, not just chasing gross adds.

On where churn "should" be: leading prepaid/MVNO brands have historically run in the 2.0%–2.5% monthly range, which leaves Boost's 2.84% with roughly 30–80 basis points of gap still to close. Because churn compounds against lifetime value, tightening the first 90 days — where the curve above is steepest — would likely be worth more to LTV than any further ARPU increase from here.

On ARPU versus churn: 2.3% ARPU growth is a reasonable number in isolation, but on its own it wasn't enough to offset the subscriber losses Boost experienced from 2020 to 2024. It only starts compounding into real revenue growth once churn stabilizes and net adds turn positive together — which is effectively what happened in 2025, when the combination helped drive a 7% year-over-year increase in wireless service revenue.

Retention strategies worth pursuing from here: tighten early-tenure churn specifically, since that's where the most value typically leaks before a subscriber's LTV has a chance to develop; keep shifting acquisition mix toward self-pay digital channels, the structurally lower-CAC, higher-quality path based on the company's own commentary; and bundle Sling TV with Boost plans. EchoStar already owns both brands and has precedent for bundling driving retention — its own Dish-Netflix pay-TV bundle measurably improved churn. A Boost+Sling bundle could work the same way on the wireless side: it raises the switching cost of leaving, since a customer would have to replace two services instead of one, and it gives EchoStar a lower-incremental-CAC acquisition channel by cross-selling into its existing Sling base instead of paid marketing.

If revenue stalls while churn and CAC look fine, where's it actually leaking?

If I were sitting in Boost's seat looking at a quarter like Q4 2025, I wouldn't take "churn is low and CAC looks fine" at face value — I'd want to know which of three things is actually happening underneath those headline numbers:

1. Mix dilution, not demand weakness. The company's own filings attribute recent net adds partly to higher Government-subsidized subscriber growth. If an increasing share of new subscribers are on lower-ARPU subsidized plans, you can grow subscriber count and lose blended ARPU at the same time — the net-adds number looks healthy while revenue per subscriber quietly erodes. The analysis needed here is an ARPU-by-acquisition-segment breakout, not just a blended company-wide average.

2. Hidden cost in the device subsidy line, not the marketing line. EchoStar explicitly cites "deeper wireless device subsidies" as a competitive pressure. A low reported CAC can mask real acquisition cost if it's being paid through subsidized hardware rather than marketing spend — the dollars just moved to a different line item. The fix is a fully-loaded CAC view that folds device subsidy cost into acquisition economics, not just marketing spend per gross add.

3. Market-share zero-sum pressure. The U.S. prepaid market is growing only modestly overall, so further growth increasingly has to come from switchers, not new-to-category customers. Taking share from a competitor is harder to do profitably than growing with the category, and it tends to come with pricing concessions that compress ARPU even while churn and CAC both look stable. This calls for win/loss and porting analysis — specifically, where are switchers coming from, and what price or offer moved them.

Here's a real fact worth flagging, though what I draw from it is my own hypothesis, not something Boost has said: in November 2025, Boost shut down its own 5G radio network and moved subscribers onto AT&T's network under a long-term agreement running through at least 2031. Boost still runs its own 5G core — billing, plans, support — but the physical network underneath is now AT&T's. Cricket is already AT&T's own wholly-owned prepaid brand, running on that same network. My read is that this makes a "strong, reliable network" pitch a weaker differentiator for Boost than it used to be, since it arguably validates Cricket's claim just as much. I could be wrong about how much this matters in practice — brand perception often lags the underlying infrastructure reality by years, and Boost may have reasons (contractual, technical, or simply different coverage maps in different markets) why the two networks don't feel equivalent to a customer even if they're the same physically. It's a hypothesis worth testing, not a settled conclusion.

If that hypothesis holds, one reasonable implication is that marketing dollars are better spent on dimensions where there's a clearer gap to exploit — price positioning, the DISH/Sling bundle (something Cricket and Metro can't replicate), the government-subsidized segment, and dealer footprint — rather than a network-reliability message that may quietly help Cricket as much as Boost. I'd want to see actual brand-perception survey data before committing real budget to this, though; it's a plausible argument, not a proven one.

What it would actually take to get to 15–17 million subscribers

At ~7.5 million subscribers today, getting to 15–17 million means roughly doubling the base — enough to clear Cricket's ~10 million and meaningfully close the gap on Metro's ~22 million. It's worth being honest about what the current trajectory actually implies: 2025's full-year net add of about 576,000 subscribers, even at the improved pace from the back half of the year, extrapolates to something like 7–8 years to add another 7.5–9.5 million subscribers organically — and that's before accounting for a quarter like Q4 2025, where growth stalled outright. Organic growth at the current run-rate alone does not get Boost there in a competitively relevant timeframe.

Closing that gap faster would realistically require some combination of: (1) a decisive segment push into the value/subsidized tier where Boost already has disclosed momentum, accepting lower ARPU in exchange for share; (2) the Sling/Boost bundle as a structural acquisition channel rather than a side promotion, cross-selling into Sling's existing base at a lower incremental CAC than paid marketing; (3) a pricing move specifically targeted at Cricket, since the two brands now share the same underlying network cost structure, which narrows the case for Cricket's modest price premium; and (4) inorganic growth — acquiring a smaller MVNO or regional prepaid brand — since EchoStar has both the capital history and the M&A appetite to do this, and it's a faster lever than organic net adds alone. Any one of these in isolation probably isn't enough; getting to 15–17 million in a reasonable window likely means doing at least two of them at once.

Showing the actual math: projecting subscribers one year out

Rather than just asserting a timeline, here's how I'd actually build it — starting from the real Q4 2025 base of 7.51 million subscribers, and two simple inputs each month: how many new subscribers come in (gross adds), and what share of the existing base leaves (churn). Net change = gross adds minus (churn rate × current base). I ran this forward 12 months under three different sets of assumptions, so you can see exactly which input is doing the work.

Getting there isn't a matter of running the current playbook a little harder; it requires changing the inputs themselves (higher gross adds, lower churn, or both) by more than any single quarter of 2025 has shown so far. I could be wrong about how achievable a step-change like that is — I don't have visibility into Boost's internal roadmap — but the arithmetic is what it is.

One plain-language point worth separating out: churn mainly affects LTV and ROI, not the payback period — payback is really just CAC divided by monthly margin, so it barely moves no matter what churn does. CAC is the lever that moves payback. That matters here because the $180 CAC I used is based on Boost's recent, relatively easy subscribers — price-sensitive switchers and the growing self-pay digital channel. The next subscriber, especially one happily sitting with Cricket or Metro already, probably costs more to win — likely requiring a bigger device subsidy or promo. That's "marginal CAC," and it's probably closer to the $230–$280 rows above than the $180 base case once Boost is actively trying to take share rather than just capture easy switchers. I don't have a disclosed figure to confirm this — it's a reasonable assumption based on how acquisition costs typically behave as a market matures, not a certainty.

Where this might actually be falling short, and simple fixes

Keeping this part simple on purpose — four plain hypotheses, each with one straightforward fix, rather than a bigger framework:

1. The network pitch may not land. If the AT&T-network overlap with Cricket is real in customers' minds, leading with "reliable network" wastes a message. Fix: lead with price and the Sling bundle instead.

2. The dealer channel may be bringing in the wrong customers. Dealer-driven subscribers appear to churn faster and cost more. Fix: keep pushing sign-ups toward the self-pay digital channel, even if growth looks slower in the short term.

3. Boost probably can't out-subsidize AT&T's own brand. Cricket's parent can likely always match a device-discount war. Fix: compete on something Cricket structurally can't copy — the Sling/DISH bundle — rather than subsidy depth.

4. Most of the value is probably leaking in the first 90 days. That's where the churn curve modeled earlier is steepest. Fix: a cheap onboarding touchpoint — a welcome text, a simple first-bill explainer — targets exactly that window, for a fraction of CAC.

Comparative Analysis

Apple, Netflix, T-Mobile, and AT&T — growth economics and the simplest path forward.

Same exercise as Boost, applied to four very different growth businesses: real numbers from their most recent filings, my own modeled CAC/LTV/payback assumptions clearly marked as mine, a simple revenue growth read, and — borrowing a discipline I associate with Steve Jobs — the single simplest lever each company actually has, stripped of complexity.

Apple

Fiscal Q3 2026 (ended June 27, 2026): revenue of $109.4 billion, up 16% year-over-year. iPhone revenue $54.3 billion (+22%), Services revenue $30.7 billion (+12%, though it missed analyst estimates). The installed base of active devices hit a new all-time high, and paid Services subscriptions passed 1.5 billion.

Apple doesn't have subscriber churn in the traditional sense, so CAC/LTV looks different here. My model: Apple's real CAC per device is low — brand pull does most of the acquisition work, not paid marketing — so hardware is close to self-funding from the first sale. The actual long-term value comes from Services revenue layered onto a device over its multi-year life. If I assume a ~4-year average device replacement cycle and modeled Services revenue of roughly $85–95 per device per year (derived from Services revenue ÷ installed base, a rough estimate since Apple doesn't disclose this directly), that's roughly $340–380 in Services LTV per device, on top of the hardware margin captured upfront. The device sale pays for itself immediately; Services is the compounding layer on top.

Revenue growth read: 16% this quarter, but Apple itself guided next quarter down to 9–11%, citing FX headwinds and supply constraints — a real deceleration, by their own admission, not my speculation.

Simplest path: stop trying to sell the next device to a new customer, and sell one more thing to the device you've already sold. With 2.5 billion-plus active devices already in hand, the growth math favors deepening Services attach over chasing incremental hardware units — the installed base is the asset; Services is just how much of it you're using.

Netflix

Q2 2026: revenue of $12.6 billion, up 13% year-over-year, with full-year guidance narrowed to $51.0–51.4 billion (12–14% growth). Ad revenue is on track to roughly double in 2026 to about $3 billion, and management says ads now account for roughly a quarter of incremental revenue growth. Netflix stopped disclosing quarterly subscriber counts and ARM (average revenue per membership) back in Q1 2025, which makes this one the hardest of the four to model cleanly — I want to flag that upfront rather than pretend otherwise.

Without disclosed churn or exact ARM, any CAC/LTV figure here is more speculative than the other three. Using a rough blended ARM estimate around $12–13/month (regional estimates run from ~$17.50 in the U.S./Canada down to ~$7 in Latin America) and an assumed monthly churn in the 2.0–2.5% range common to mature streaming services, modeled average lifetime would run 40–50 months, implying an LTV somewhere in the $350–450 range at a ~35% content-adjusted margin. I'd treat that range as a rough sketch, not a confident number — Netflix's own disclosure gap is the real story here.

Revenue growth read: a clear, gradual deceleration — 18% in Q4 2025, down to 16% in Q1 2026, down to 13% in Q2 2026 — consistent with a maturing subscriber base where pricing and ads are doing more of the growth work than new membership volume.

Simplest path: stop optimizing for new subscribers and optimize for hours watched per existing one. Netflix's moat was never subscriber count — it's that once you're in, there's always something queued up. More engagement from the people already paying is a more reliable lever than finding new ones in an increasingly saturated market.

T-Mobile

Q2 2026: postpaid net account additions of 277,000 (down 13% year-over-year off a tough comp), postpaid ARPA of $152.91 (+2%), postpaid phone churn of just 0.85% — down meaningfully year-over-year and the lowest of any major U.S. carrier this quarter. Postpaid service revenue grew 13% to $15.9 billion. Management flagged Q3 net adds dropping to roughly 250,000 due to planned rate-plan changes temporarily lifting churn.

At 0.85% monthly phone churn, modeled average account lifetime runs about 118 months — nearly a decade. Applying a ~40% contribution margin to the $152.91 ARPA gives a modeled LTV near $7,200 per account. T-Mobile doesn't disclose CAC, so I modeled $500 as a reasonable assumption for blended acquisition cost per account; that implies a payback period of roughly 8 months and an LTV:CAC ratio over 14x — an extremely favorable modeled economics, which matches how durable the underlying churn number actually is.

Revenue growth read: postpaid service revenue growth (13%) is outpacing net add growth, meaning the gains are increasingly coming from existing accounts spending more, not just new ones — a healthier growth mix than pure volume.

Simplest path: T-Mobile already found this one and it's worth naming directly — "Un-carrier." One clear, simple promise (no contracts, no hidden fees, better value) repeated for over a decade, instead of a new complicated pitch every year. The lesson generalizes: pick one honest advantage and don't dilute it.

AT&T

Q2 2026: 432,000 postpaid phone net adds (beating estimates of roughly 338,500), postpaid phone churn of 0.86%, postpaid phone ARPU of $58.01, wireless service revenue of $17.41 billion. Converged customers — those taking both wireless and home internet from AT&T — reached 6.0 million, a 42.5% convergence rate, and AT&T's own management stated plainly that converged customers churn less and carry higher lifetime value.

At 0.86% monthly churn, modeled average subscriber lifetime runs about 116 months. On a per-phone-line basis (not the multi-line account basis T-Mobile reports), applying a ~40% margin to the $58.01 ARPU gives a modeled LTV near $2,690. Using an assumed CAC of $450, that's a payback period of about 19 months and an LTV:CAC near 6x — solidly positive, but structurally lower than T-Mobile's per-account economics simply because this is a per-line, not per-account, comparison; the two aren't directly apples-to-apples.

Revenue growth read: net adds are beating expectations and churn is stable, but AT&T itself is explicit that convergence — not wireless alone — is the growth engine; wireless-only churn resistance is real but smaller than the churn reduction convergence delivers.

Simplest path: one bill, one network, one reason to never call a competitor. AT&T's CEO put it in four words on the Q2 call — "Where we have fiber, we win" — which is really just convergence restated as simply as possible. The strategy isn't complicated; it's just under-deployed relative to AT&T's footprint.

Revenue, subscriber, churn, and ARPU/ARPA figures for all four companies are sourced from their most recent SEC filings and earnings releases (Apple's fiscal Q3 2026, Netflix's and T-Mobile's and AT&T's calendar Q2 2026). CAC, LTV, payback period, and the modeled device/Services economics for Apple are my own assumptions, built where the companies don't disclose the underlying figures directly — Netflix's numbers in particular should be read as a rough sketch given the company no longer reports subscriber counts or ARM. The "simplest path" framing for each company is my own synthesis, not a statement from any of these companies.
